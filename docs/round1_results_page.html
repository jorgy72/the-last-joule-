The Last Joule

Agents with a fixed energy budget answered noisy questions, paid for every step of thinking, and died if they ran dry. Evolution chose how they spent. Three predictions went in. Two came out roughly right, and one came out backwards.

Agents that could read their meter wasted less and answered more questions right than agents that couldn't. Scrambling the meter wiped out the advantage. Cutting it after evolution dropped survival from 96% to 57%.

PREDICTION 2
Failed

The coherent share of spending fell as reserves ran down, from 87% near full to 71% near empty. Scarcity made the agent less coherent per joule, not more.

PREDICTION 3
Partly held

Near empty, the agent didn't save its last joules for a careful answer. It switched to fast, cheap guesses that kept it alive. Giving up on hard questions never evolved.

The world

Each agent lives through up to 400 questions. Every question has a hidden yes-or-no answer behind noisy evidence. One step of thinking draws one more piece of evidence. The agent keeps thinking until the evidence clears its threshold, then answers.

An agent's whole strategy is a few numbers. One sets how sure it needs to be before answering. One lets that threshold change with its meter reading. Two more let it give up on a question that looks hard after one step. Evolution tuned these numbers separately under each set of rules, with three independent runs each, and every agent was then tested on 5,000 fresh lifetimes it had never seen.

The coherent share is the energy spent on questions that ended in a correct answer, divided by all the energy spent thinking. It is the toy version of coherent energy over total energy.

1. Does sensing the meter help?

Yes, by a small but clear margin, and only when the meter carries real information.

The two bottom rows show why stakes matter. The agent evolved with energy as a plain cost learned to think very little, and its strategy barely changes with its reserves. The agent evolved with a meter and nothing at stake learned to think as long as it was allowed to, every time. Dropped into the real world, it was dead within five questions on average.

Only death at zero made the reading on the meter matter. Behavioural ecologists have long found the same thing in foraging animals: choices start to depend on reserves when running out means starving.

2. Does coherence rise as the budget shrinks?

No. It falls. The agent that reads its meter thinks hard when it's full and cuts corners when it's low, so it gets more questions wrong and more of its spending is wasted.

Two readings are possible. As tested, the prediction failed. But your framework also maps pain onto metabolic crisis and decoherence, and that mapping matches this curve: coherence drops as the system nears crisis. That reading came after seeing the data, so it needs its own test.

Over a whole life, the meter-reading agent still had the higher coherent share (83.9% against 81.7% for the agent with no meter). And a high ratio alone means little: the agent that thinks the longest had the best ratio of all (95.9%), and starved within five questions. The ratio only tracks something useful alongside staying alive.

3. What does it do with its last joules?

It goes cheap. At full reserves the evolved agent spends about five steps on a question. Near empty it spends one or two, and takes the 70-to-75% chance of a quick guess.

The option to abandon a hard-looking question was there in every run and never evolved. With a living cost of 1.10 J per question, skipping still costs energy and earns nothing, so a cheap guess is always the better bet in this world. A world where resting is cheaper than guessing might evolve a different last move.

What this shows, and what it doesn't

  • It shows that sensing your own energy only pays off when running out has a real cost, and that an agent evolved under those rules comes to depend on the reading. That's the functional loop from the video: sense, act, spend, sense.
  • It does not show anything about feeling. These agents are a handful of numbers. The simulation tests whether the framework's energy ratio behaves the way the framework predicts. It says nothing on whether anything is felt.
  • The coherent share here is one simple operational choice. A different definition, such as counting only the steps that changed the answer, would move with thinking length and could make prediction 2 come out the other way for reasons that have nothing to do with the framework. That's why this one was used.
  • The world's numbers were set so the budget actually bites. In an easier world (lower living cost), nobody dies, and the meter stops mattering.
  • The fractal-dimension term from the full framework has no counterpart here.
